Age and contact matrices

Measured contact patterns predict the immunity factor without fitting to epidemic outcomes: 1.1-1.7 from age alone, 2.3-3.0 with one-day contact diaries, and 3.4-6.8 from repeated surveys of the same people, depending on how very high contact counts are capped.

Age alone

Let $M_{ij}$ be the average number of daily contacts a person of age group $i$ has with people of age group $j$, from the synthetic matrices of Prem et al. (2021). The infected fraction $x_i$ of age group $i$ grows as

$$\dot x_i = \beta\,s_i\,\sigma_i\sum_j M_{ij}\,x_j - \gamma\,x_i,$$

where $s_i$ is the susceptible share and $\sigma_i$ a relative susceptibility.

Early infections take the age profile $v$, the leading right eigenvector of $\operatorname{diag}(\sigma)M$. Let $u$ be its left eigenvector and $n_i$ the population shares. Depleting the susceptibles along $v$ lowers the leading eigenvalue at the rate

$$\lambda_{\text{age}} = \frac{\sum_i u_i v_i^2 \,\big/ \sum_i u_i v_i}{\sum_i n_i v_i} .$$

The numerator is how strongly early infections sit in the groups that drive transmission. The denominator converts to the overall infected share.

An immunity factor of 2.3 to 3.0, from contact surveys alone, matches what epidemics show where control was weak: 2 to 3.5 in US county waves in winter 2020-21, and 2.5 in Manaus.

A one-day diary mixes lasting differences between people with day-to-day variation, so the fixed-activity value is an upper bound for the lasting part. POLYMOD also under-records very high contact counts in some countries, which pushes the other way.

With activity held fixed, the final size in Brazil at $R = 1.6$ would be 35%. The reported attack in Manaus, 66-76%, is difficult to reconcile with this fixed-activity example. Changing activity is one possible explanation (activity that changes over time); the contact distribution in Manaus and the blood-donor estimates are additional uncertainties.

How long busy phases last

A one-day diary cannot tell a busy person from a busy day.

The CoMix survey (CoMix UK) ran in the United Kingdom from March 2020 to March 2022, and each participant reported their contacts up to eight or ten times, two weeks apart. The median adult is observed over 123 days and 90% within 267 days; about 4% rejoin after a gap of around seven months.

For 21,158 adults seen at least three times, activity is the number of contacts on the diary day divided by the mean for the same week and age group. That removes the common effect of lockdowns and seasons on each age group, though not effects that differ between people.

The covariance of a person's activity between surveys separated by $\ell$ days is fitted as

$$C(\ell) = \sigma_p^2 + \sigma_s^2\,e^{-\ell/\tau} \quad (\ell \ge 7\ \text{days}), \qquad C(0) = \sigma_p^2 + \sigma_s^2 + \sigma_n^2 ,$$

with a persistent part $\sigma_p^2$, a switching part $\sigma_s^2$ with correlation time $\tau$, and a residual $\sigma_n^2$ that repeat observations a week or more apart cannot resolve:

Under this covariance model, 26-30% of the variance of capped daily contacts is persistent and 13-14% belongs to a component with a correlation time of 23 to 36 days.

The remaining 57-61% is unresolved by repeat observations a week or more apart. It includes day-to-day variation, and it may also include genuine correlations lasting less than a week, which could matter over an infectious period. The shares are similar under the three caps; the levels are not.

During a wave growing at rate $r$, the persistent part acts in full and the switching part in proportion $r/(r + 1/\tau)$; the unresolved residual is left out. With the UK age matrix, the predicted immunity factor, without fitting to epidemic outcomes, is

The level depends on the cap: 3.4-3.7 at 20, 4.6-5.2 at 50 and 5.9-6.8 at 100. The CoMix team caps at 50 contacts a day, the value they found to track changes in the reproduction number most closely (Gimma et al. 2022). How many of a very busy person's contacts can transmit is unresolved.

The US county waves of winter 2020-21 imply 2.4-5.3 over fatality rates from 0.5% to 1%, and Gomes et al. (2022) fit about 3.5 to England and Scotland. At $R = 2.5$, $\lambda = 3.5$ puts the peak near 23% and $\lambda = 5$ near 17%.

The immunity factor changes by only about 7% across a tenfold range of growth rates. That is why the county data could not measure how the turnover varies with growth.

The persistent covariance is flat from one month to the longest lags, so it behaves like a habit over the months each person is observed; restricting the fit to lags of at most 200 days changes each share by at most one point. In this model the transient part is small and decorrelates over about a month, and the persistent part is two to three times larger. That describes a mostly lasting reduction in the threshold, which is difficult to reconcile with the attack reported for Manaus.

CoMix was collected during the pandemic, when contacts were fewer and more unequal than usual, and contact counts are an imperfect proxy for transmission.
